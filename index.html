FOLLOW THE SUBSTITUTION

One example,
step by step.

Example check · not theorem verification

1. Convert physical mass to geometric length.

m = GM/c²

2. Compute the spherical horizon area.

A = 16πm²

3. Substitute that area into the lower bound.

√(A/16π) = √(m²) = m, since m > 0

4. Convert the bound back to solar masses.

M ≥ (c²/G)m = M

The analytic substitution proves equality for this established Schwarzschild example. The displayed numbers check that example with rounded readouts. The broader proposed theorem requires its manuscript assumptions and is not verified by this visualization. See exactly what is formalized ↗

READ THE GEOMETRY

What the picture

01 / THE SLICE

Curvature is about distances.

On a constant-time equatorial slice, radial distances are stretched relative to Euclidean space. The mesh represents that two-dimensional metric as a surface in three dimensions.

ds² = dr² / (1 − 2m/r) + r² dφ²
z = +√[8m(r − 2m)],   r > 2m

The rim at r = 2m is the limiting boundary. The extra embedding direction is not an additional physical dimension. Only one exterior sheet is shown.

02 / THE HORIZON

The area wraps a sphere.

The horizon has areal radius 2m. Its surface area is 4π(2m)² = 16πm². A disk drawn across the mesh’s rim would have only π(2m)² — one quarter of the spherical area.

A = 16πm²
Double M → double rs → four times A

The sphere view is a separate representation of the horizon’s intrinsic area, not a sphere sitting inside the embedding surface.

03 / THE BOUND

Area sets a mass floor.

Under the relevant manuscript assumptions, the proposed Penrose inequality gives m ≥ √(Amin/16π), where Amin denotes the appropriate least enclosing area. In the Schwarzschild equality example, Amin = 16πm².

m ≥ √(Amin / 16π)
M ≥ (c²/G) √(Amin / 16π)

Dividing all lengths by m makes every positive-mass Schwarzschild shape coincide. Changing mass changes its physical size.
